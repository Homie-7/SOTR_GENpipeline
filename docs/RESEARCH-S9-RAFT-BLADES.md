1818 account gives no names (see 2.4).

## 2. History (from Savigny & Corréard, 1817/1818)

### 2.1 The raft and the boarding (5 July 1816)

- About 147 to 150 people went on the raft: "one hundred and twenty soldiers, including the officers of
  the army, twenty-nine men, sailors and passengers, and **one woman**."
- **Weapons at the boarding (documented):** the soldiers "wanted to take their muskets and some
  cartridges: this was formally opposed. They left them on the deck, and preserved only their sabres:
  some few, however, saved their carbines, and, almost all the officers, their fowling pieces and pistols."
  → **The muskets stayed on the frigate.** So the soldiers' bayonets on the raft were loose blades held in
  the hand, not fixed to muskets.
- The build: top-masts, yards and booms of the frigate, lashed with "very strong ropes", planks nailed on
  top, a side rail "not above forty centimetres in height". The raft was about 20 m x 7 m (the commonly
  quoted figure; the narrative gives it in metres and toises).
- **Mast and ropes:** after the tow was cut, Savigny set up a mast from a cut spar and the main-top-gallant
  sail. "The mast was kept up by the rope which had served to tow us, of which we made shrouds and stays."
  It stood on the front third of the raft. A bag of money (1,500 francs) hung on it.

### 2.2 First night (5-6 July)

- A storm. Men were trapped with their legs between the timbers. About 20 died or were lost. No mutiny yet.

### 2.3 Second night (6-7 July) — the great mutiny

- The soldiers broke into a wine cask. They planned to kill the officers and to cut the ropes of the raft.
- **The boarding axe:** "One of them advanced to the edge of the raft with a **boarding-axe**, and began to
  strike the cords: this was the signal for revolt." He was "the first victim: a blow with a sabre put an
  end to his existence."
- **Who had what (documented):** "The mutineers drew their **sabres**, and those who had none, armed
  themselves with **knives**." They presented "a front bristling with **sabres and bayonets**." Later, "men
  presented themselves, armed with **knives, sabres and bayonets**; many had **carbines, which they used as
  clubs**." One man cut the cords "with a knife". Mutineers tried "to cut out his eyes with a **penknife**".
  The officers' side "with our sabres drawn ... traversed the lines which the soldiers formed."
- **The mast fell:** "a crowd of madmen ... cut the stays, and let the mast fall." It was put up again the
  next day.
- **Moonlight (documented):** after the mutineers were pushed back, "The moon with her sad beams, illumined
  this fatal raft". The fighting happened in the dark ("Notwithstanding the darkness we found the wound").
- **The woman, first time:** the mutineers threw "the unfortunate woman ... into the sea, as well as her
  husband, who defended her with courage." Corréard tied a rope round himself, jumped in and saved her.
  The husband and wife "had seen themselves attacked with **sabres and bayonets**". She prayed aloud to
  "our Lady of Laux".
- A second attack came "nearly midnight": the soldiers "rushed upon us like madmen, with their knives or
  sabres in their hands." Savigny took a knife wound in the right arm. 60 to 65 died in the night.

### 2.4 The cantinière (documented)

- She was from the Hautes-Alpes, near Gap (Notre-Dame du Laus / "Our Lady of Laux"). She left home "24
  years before". She had served in "the Campaigns in Italy, &c. as a **sutler**" (cantinière / vivandière)
  and "had never quitted our armies". She said: "preserve my life, you see that I am a useful woman."
- She sold food and brandy to the troops on credit. She gave Corréard a little snuff, "all she possessed".
- **No name** is given for her or for her husband in the account. "Agnès" and "Henri" are the play's.
- She was **thrown into the sea a second time** in the third-night plot ("In this confusion the unfortunate
  woman was, a second time, thrown into the sea"). Coudin and some workmen pulled her out again.

### 2.5 Third and fourth nights

- A plot of soldiers and some Black soldiers to throw the officers into the sea. A Spaniard held the mast,
  crossed himself, "and held a **knife** in the other" hand. An Italian servant "armed himself with **the
  last boarding-axe that there was on the raft**" and threw himself into the sea. The second mention means
  there was more than one boarding axe at the start, and none after this.
- Cannibalism of the dead started on about day 4 (documented in the account).
- By the fifth dawn, 30 were left.

### 2.6 Day 7 — the killing of the sick ("the fifteen") — documented

- Two soldiers drank from the last wine barrel with a reed. They were "thrown into the sea" under the
  death law the group had sworn. The boy Léon (12) died the same day.
- 27 were left; "but fifteen seemed likely to live some days". After a debate "it was resolved to throw
  them [the sick] into the sea."
- **"Three sailors and a soldier took on themselves this cruel execution: we turned our faces aside."**
- **"Among them were the unfortunate woman and her husband."** She "had a thigh broken between the pieces
  of wood composing the raft, and her husband had received a deep wound with a **sabre** on his head."
- "This French woman, to whom soldiers and Frenchmen gave the sea for a tomb, had partaken for twenty years
  in the glorious fatigues of our armies".
- **After the cull, "we threw the arms into the sea; we reserved, however, one sabre** in case it should be
  wanted to cut a rope or piece of wood." So the cull is the last moment when many blades are on the raft.
- The time of day of the cull is **not stated** (the wine theft was "in the course of the day").
- The 15 were rescued by the brig *Argus* on 17 July 1816 (13 days). Five died soon after at Saint-Louis.

### 2.7 Documented vs. legend / dramatic licence

## 3. Blade silhouettes table

Shadow notes for all rows: a small object close to a point light throws a large, soft-edged shadow; far
from the light it throws a small, hard one. Moonlight is a distant source, so a true moonlit shadow is
near true size and hard-edged. For horror scale, the theatre convention is a near lamp (1819-correct: an
Argand oil lamp or a lantern). A blade seen **edge-on** to the light becomes a thin line; seen **flat** it
shows its full profile. The hilt (guard, pommel) is what tells the viewer which weapon it is.

Measurements from dealer and auction listings are approximate (±1 to 2 cm).

**Not to use (too late or wrong):** French boarding sabre M1833 and boarding axe M1833 (1833); British 1804
cutlass (wrong navy); any sword bayonet / yataghan bayonet (1840s on); a bayonet fixed on a musket (muskets
were left on the frigate); pistols-as-shadow are possible (documented for officers) but are not blades.

**Design reading of the history for the four stills (a suggestion, not a decision):** the account names four
executioners, three sailors and one soldier. One possible mapping: still 1 the soldier's briquet (the
script's "sabre"); stills 2-4 sailors' blades (clasp knife, boarding axe, loose bayonet passed hand to hand).
Each still then has a different hilt shape, so the four images read as four hands, not one.

### Shadow and projection horror that an 1819 audience knew

- **Robertson's Fantasmagorie**, Paris, from 1797/98 (Pavillon de l'Échiquier), then about six years in the
  former Capuchin convent near Place Vendôme. A magic lantern on wheels (the "fantascope") behind a screen,
  so figures grew and rushed at the audience; total darkness, smoke, thunder, bells. Ghosts and skeletons on
  black. The rush-forward "grow" is the 1819-true way to make a shadow loom.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phantasmagoria · Commons: `File:Fantasmagorie de Robertson.tif`
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fantasmagorie_de_Robertson.tif · `File:Mémoires récréatifs ... Robertson (vol. 1 frontispiece).jpg`
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:%C3%89tienne-Gaspard_Robert
- **Philidor's "Phantasmagorie"**, Paris, Dec 1792 - Jul 1793 (first use of the word). Same Wikipedia page.
- **Séraphin's ombres chinoises** (shadow theatre), Versailles from 1772, Palais-Royal from 1784, still
  running in 1819: cut-out silhouettes behind a lit screen. The Paris model for flat black profile figures.
  https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9%C3%A2tre_S%C3%A9raphin (URL not opened in this research; verify)
