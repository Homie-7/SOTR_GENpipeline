The short version

Scene 9 is the script's “four-way split”: the raft, the court, the salon and Géricault's studio, each taking the stage in turn. The raft is a physical riser, so my job is the other three worlds, one per projection surface:

SurfaceWorldBeatsWhat the audience sees
Right flatMarie-Louise's salon2–3, 8A candlelit Rococo wall with Gérard's portrait of Louis XVIII. The candles light one by one, and they snuff out when the court takes over.
Back wall (centre)The court martial3, 5, 7The Judge as a black cut-paper silhouette on a lit paper screen. He grows bigger with each appearance, and his gavel strikes toward the audience.
Left flatGéricault's studio4, 6, 9One candle at 2 a.m. and a huge canvas where The Raft of the Medusa is painted, stroke by stroke, as he talks. It is finished at beat 9.

Every file is a finished piece of picture and sound. The only thing the show operator does is fade each file in and out. Each world also breaks apart on the side facing the other walls, the whole time it is lit, so the three read as fragments of reality rather than three rectangles.

How the stage shapes everything

The RMIT Black Box set is three surfaces, like an open book facing the audience: a left flat and a right flat, each 4.8 × 3.6 m and angled 25°, and the back wall, 6 × 3.6 m, between them. These rules followed from that, and they explain most of what you see:

  • Walls as walls. Each surface shows one wall of the room, square-on and at true scale, as if the actors were standing in the room. The bottom of the picture is the stage floor, so a dado rail sits at 0.9 m and a sconce at 1.9 m, and an actor walking up to the wall stands at the right size against it. No floor is painted, because the real floor is the floor.
  • The camera never moves. No pans, pushes or drift. The room can't move behind a live actor. All motion happens inside the picture: flames, smoke, paint, the break.
  • No people in any picture. The actors are real. The only figure is the Judge, and he is an animated silhouette.
  • One world per surface for its whole run. Salon on the right, studio on the left, court on the back wall (confirmed by the client). That cut the build from six wall pictures to two, plus the court.
  • Nothing newer than 1819. The wreck was 1816, the court martial 1817, and the painting was shown in 1819. France is Bourbon again (Louis XVIII), so there are no Napoleonic motifs anywhere.
  • Finished files, no playback fixes. Joins, holds, loops and smoke are built into the files. We never rely on QLab or the mapping software to finish the picture. The operator only fades each file in and out.

The three worlds, beat by beat

Beat lengths are estimated from the script's word count at stage pace (about 2.3 words a second, a little slower for the Judge's declaiming), plus the written stage business. They will be re-timed after rehearsal.

Right flat The salon

The script asks for

“An ornate Louis XIV Salon,” “slowly revealed.” Marie-Louise reads Corréard's letter and laughs at the news (beat 2). The salon dims when the court starts (beat 3). At beat 8 she is back: “Mais bien sûr, dear Chaumareys cannot be punished!”

What I built

Beats 2–3 (1:57): dusk, then the candles light one after another and the gilt catches first. The lit room holds through the letter. At the court's first gavel the candles snuff, left then right, with thin smoke from the wicks, and the room sits in dusk while the Judge speaks. Beat 8 (0:16): the room snaps back already lit, then snuffs again as its exit.

Why it looks like this

  • White and gold panelling, crimson only in the silk. A light room keeps it clearly apart from the dark studio. Red walls would fight the studio's blood-red, and dark damask disappears at projector black levels.
  • An old pre-Revolution room still in use in 1817. A royalist in a surviving Rococo room is historically right, since the Restoration was nostalgic for exactly that. It's also what “ornate Louis XIV” reads as at a glance.
  • The portrait is Louis XVIII, the king who “loves his loyal subjects,” hanging behind her while she reads. The painting itself is François Gérard's real coronation portrait (public domain), composited in, because image generators paint their own wrong king.
  • Beat 8 has no slow reveal: it's only about 9 seconds long. A reveal would eat the line, and the salon being instantly, smugly at home is the point.
  • The candles are calm on purpose. The salon's many candles are composed, while the studio's single candle struggles. It's the same light source behaving in opposite ways.
  • The chandelier and the mirror were dropped. The script never asks for them, and they would only have appeared on walls we never project.

Left flat Géricault's studio

The script asks for

Géricault working at a half-completed canvas, pulling a rotting head from a hessian bag (“I must have the likeness,” beat 4); raging “I will show it all!” (beat 6); stepping back with Sarah to look at the finished painting (“Où est l'humanité?”, beat 9).

What I built

A bare studio at 2 a.m., lit by one candle, with a huge canvas. The Raft of the Medusa is painted in, brushstroke by brushstroke, over the first ~20 s of each beat: about half at beat 4, most of it at beat 6, and complete at beat 9, with the waving man at the top painted last.

Why it looks like this

  • The painting follows the script, not a formula. The script says “half-completed” at beat 4, so there is no blank or sketch stage. The unpainted parts show how Géricault really worked: the composition outlined in brown on the primed canvas, painted figure by figure.
  • The order has meaning. The dead and dying go in first, then the heap of bodies and the sail. The man waving at the horizon, the hope, is the very last thing painted, as Géricault and Sarah step back to look: “made visible for all to see.”
  • Brushstrokes, not a fade. The strokes follow the forms of the painting: broad in the sky and sea, smaller on the figures. He is visibly painting while he talks, and the growth isn't tied to where his brush is, so it works with any blocking.
  • The painting is the real 1819 work, composited and lit by the scene's own candle. The canvas is scaled to 3.2 × 4.7 m (the original is 4.9 × 7.2 m) so the whole composition fits the wall, including the figure at the top. That was a deliberate liberty.
  • One candle is the only light, flickering in a draught. It's the opposite of the salon's many calm candles.
  • No body parts in the picture: the rotting head is a real prop in the actor's hands, and the audience is 13+.

Back wall The court martial

The script asks for

“Only the Judge is animated,” in silhouette, reading his findings “like a town crier.” “Order! Order!” and the gavel (beat 3). Findings Two and Three, the gallery shouting, “Get the TRAITORS out!” as he loses control (beat 5). The verdict: “An Act of God… CAN NOT be held responsible” (beat 7).

What I built

A faceless naval officer cut from black paper, on a lit paper screen with soft shadows of a courtroom behind it. Beat 3: about 2 m tall, one strike. Beat 5: bigger on “Two!”, filling the wall on “Three!”, striking again. Beat 7: close on his head and chest for the verdict, then one last blow where the gavel alone fills the wall, then black.

Why it looks like this

  • A naval officer, not a robed judge. Chaumareys was tried by a naval court martial, a panel of officers in uniform. A robe and cap would be the wrong institution.
  • Facing the audience, not in profile. In profile, a bicorne, tailcoat and sword read instantly as Napoleon, and this is the Bourbon state. Facing out also puts the accusation on the audience, who sit where the public gallery sat.
  • No hat and no face. Every hat came back as Napoleon's. A bare, featureless head is the most honest image anyway: the court is a faceless institution. He has no mouth because his voice is pre-recorded (the actor also plays Sarah, who is on stage).
  • He grows across his three appearances, because the script gives him exactly three (the studio takes the back wall in between). The jumps are hard cuts on the gavel: authority doesn't fade in.
  • The gavel comes at the audience, not down. A downward strike needs a bench and depth, and on a flat black silhouette it vanished into the body twice. Toward the audience reads, and it lands the blow on them.
  • The gavel is licence. French courts used a bell or a voice, but the script writes “the gavel,” and the play's version is canon.
  • The last blow, where the gavel fills the wall, is my addition (not in the script). It's a physical full stop on the verdict before the salon's smug reply. It's worth confirming with the director.
  • One judge, not three. The script has one Judge with one pre-recorded voice, so three bodies would read as a mistake. Each appearance gets its own size and framing instead.
  • The paper screen, not a flat white. A dimmer, warmer paper with a lamp breathing behind it and grey courtroom shadows follows the 19th-century shadow-theatre tradition. It stops the back wall looking flat and too bright next to the two lit rooms.

The breaking edges

No wall is a hard rectangle. Each world is whole on its outer side and breaks up toward the centre of the stage, like the Raft dissolving in the 2022 Slide 9 concept. The break is on for the whole time a wall is lit, and the show plays these versions.

WorldHow it breaksWhy that way
Salon (left edge)Blocks of the panelling crack away and float off into the dark, Control-style.A room is a physical space, so it breaks like masonry. The break stops short of the left sconce, which still lights and snuffs.
Studio (right edge)The plaster beside the canvas breaks into blocks that drift away.Same logic as the salon. The canvas never breaks: it isn't masonry, and the painting is the point of the scene.
Court (both edges)The paper screen burns away: a glowing char line creeps slowly inward from beat 3 to beat 7, and ash and sparks drift off. The Judge never burns.The court's world is paper, and paper disintegrates by burning. The official lie is being consumed at its edges while the Judge still holds his shape.

The break only ever moves one way. An earlier version played a short generated clip forwards then backwards, so the pieces broke off and then came back together, which read as a cheap reverse loop. Now the broken edge is there from the first frame, pieces keep coming away and drifting off, and nothing ever reassembles. Where a loop is needed (the backup HOLD pieces), it cycles the flow of pieces like a waterfall and never rewinds.

How they were made: the salon and studio breaks were generated in Higgsfield (Seedance 2.5, an “edit video” pass that breaks the wall while keeping the framing exactly), then carried onto the finished files by script. The court's burn is built entirely by script from its own paper screen, with no generation. The smoke from the salon's snuffed candles is also carried over the broken area, so it rises continuously across the gaps.

Things tried and dropped

Worth knowing in case someone suggests them again:

  • A robed judge, a profile judge, a judge in a hat: the wrong institution, then Napoleon, then Napoleon again.
  • A “tribunal of three” for the verdict: the script has one Judge. Reverted.
  • A downward gavel strike: it vanished into the silhouette (twice). Replaced by the strike toward the audience.
  • New strike animations for beats 3 and 5: the approved strike was already “spot on.” Kept that one.
  • Torn-paper edges on the court: they read as shreds. Floating cubes on the court: masonry doesn't belong on paper. Replaced by burning.
  • Breaking the painting's canvas: tried and dropped. The canvas isn't a physical space, and the painting should stay whole.
  • The back-and-forth (“ping-pong”) loop on the edges: it read as cheap. Replaced by the one-way flow.
  • An opacity fade for the painting: replaced by brushstrokes.

Likely questions

Why doesn't the Judge have a face?

The court is a faceless institution. Practically, every attempt at a face or hat turned him into Napoleon, which is wrong for the Bourbon state. His voice is pre-recorded because the same actor plays Sarah, who is on stage, so nothing needs a mouth.

Why is the painting smaller than the real one?

At true scale (4.9 × 7.2 m) only its bottom 3.6 m would fit the wall, and we'd lose the figure waving at the top, the image beat 9 is built on. Scaling it to 3.2 × 4.7 m keeps the whole composition. It was a deliberate choice.

Why do the walls break apart?

Scene 9 is a four-way split of realities. Each world is whole on its outer side and breaks up toward the centre of the stage, where the others are. It grows out of the 2022 concept's dissolving Raft. The rooms break like masonry, and the court's paper screen burns, because each breaks the way its material would.

Can the lengths change after rehearsal?

Yes. The files are built from loops, so they can be re-cut to any length without regenerating anything. The backup pieces also let an operator follow the actors live.

Is this AI-generated?

The room pictures, the candle loops, the snuff and the Judge's strike were generated in Higgsfield from carefully specified prompts. The two paintings are the real public-domain works. The timing, joins, painting-in, edges, burn and sound are built by script, so they are exact and repeatable.

What does the operator have to do?

Fade each file in at the start and out at the end. Everything else (loops, joins, smoke, the break) is already in the file.
