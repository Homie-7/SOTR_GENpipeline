# BIBLE — Secret of the Raft

Canon for the play, as far as the projection work needs it. This is a summary. The
workshop script (`2026 RMIT Dev 'Secret of the Raft' Workhop Draft V1.pdf`, © Louise Howlett /
ReAction Theatre) is the authority. It is copyrighted, so it stays out of this repo; see
`docs/SOURCES.md`.

## The play

A bilingual English/French play by **Louise Howlett**, dramaturgy by **Matthew Whittet**.
Runs about 60 minutes, for audiences aged 13 and up. Developed at the RMIT Black Box, 2026.

**Premise.** Sarah, 15, is at the Louvre with her mother Ann. She would rather be on her
phone. She slips on a strange puddle in front of Géricault's *The Raft of the Medusa* and
hits her head. In her unconscious mind, the painting floods the gallery. Amina, the museum
cleaner, guides her back through the story of **Agnès**, a cantinière (a woman attached to
Napoleon's army as a canteen keeper) who was on the raft, died on it, and was left out of
the painting.

**Thesis.** Who gets remembered, and who decides. Agnès is "unseen": left out of the
painting, left off the lifeboats, and written out by the court and by society. Sarah's
closing line is the whole play: *"They all forgot you… but I won't."*

## Scene 6 — The Napoleonic Wars (next, from 2026-09-28)

**What the client asked (2026-09-28, via Homie):** "essentially a movement scene with fragmented
spoken word… float some imagery over the movement rather than create a fully developed
environment." Imagery they had considered: maps of Africa, the French flag, people fleeing Africa,
other fragments tied to the movement and the text. Visual refs: `INSPO/2026 Visual References - SOTR.pdf`
(pp. 7, 8, 13) and the 2022 deck's Slide 6 (an old map of Africa with "1816" written over it,
documents scattered on the walls and floor).

**What the script has.** Out of Scene 5's ending (a cannonball: "Smoke and darkness cover the camp"),
Sarah and Amina as two Napoleonic soldiers and Agnès as the cantinière come out of the battle haze
upstage, march downstage centre in line to military music and form **battle tableaux**: running
beside the men, serving cognac under fire, tending the wounded. Over it, **a bilingual echo**: Agnès
speaks French, Amina repeats it in English, overlapping ("-" = no pause). 188 spoken words, so about
80 s of speech; the scene runs roughly 2½–3 min with the march, the tableaux and the silences.

**Scene 7** (the Medusa's deck, the map projected on the sail) is **Sahaj's**; he gets our renders and
matches to them (Homie, 2026-09-28). **Music and sound are the sound designer's**; our picture leads
the timing and the timecodes go to him (same as Scene 9).

**Era, Scene 6: nothing newer than 1816** (Homie approved 2026-09-28). Agnès's "twenty-three years"
are 1792 to Waterloo, June 1815; the Medusa sails in June 1816. Sourced imagery is dated 1816 or
earlier (engravings of the battles, Goya's *Disasters of War*, French maps of 1707-1749, Baugean's
frigate). The Corréard plates of the raft are 1818 and stay out of this scene.

**"People fleeing"** is done as cut-paper silhouettes, the court's language: no faces, no photographs
of real refugees (Homie approved 2026-09-28). They read as the exiles of 1815 and as today's at once.
In the script Agnès flees France *toward* Africa; the client's photos point at people fleeing
Africa by sea today, the raft's modern mirror. The silhouettes hold both without choosing.

## Scene 9 — The Aftermath

The script calls it "a four-way split scene": Agnès on the raft, the Judge in silhouette
(an animation), Marie-Louise in her salon, and Géricault in his studio. "Each section
transforms in turn." Sarah moves between them as a listener or a silent witness.

Running order of the fragments:

| Beat | World | What happens | Tone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | RAFT | Agnès at night, moonlight, clutching her one biscuit. It's torn from her. | Terror |
| 2 | **SALON** | "Slowly revealed." Marie-Louise reads Corréard's letter to other salonnières, laughs: "Good riddance to the riff-raff." Baroque minuet, harpsichord. | Contempt, elegance |
| 3 | **COURT** | Courtroom hubbub. "Order! Order!" The gavel. Sarah jumps. Judge reads finding **One!** | Official, pompous |
| 4 | **STUDIO** | Géricault paints; pulls a rotting head from a hessian bag to show Sarah. "I must have the likeness." | Obsession, horror |
| 5 | **COURT** | Gavel. Finding **Two!** The public gallery shouts "You're lying!" Finding **Three!**, a scuffle, "Get the TRAITORS out!" The judge loses control. | Escalating |
| 6 | **STUDIO** | "Twenty were murdered… Hunger. Thirst. Madness. Cannibalism. I will show it all!" | Rage |
| 7 | **COURT** | Yelling now. "An Act of God… CAN NOT be held responsible." | Verdict, the lie |

## Who's in Scene 9

Actors double roles. Only the Judge is animated; everyone else is a live performer in
front of the plates.

- **The JUDGE**: animated, in silhouette. Reads his findings "like a town crier." Played
  by the actor who plays Sarah, so his voice must be **pre-recorded**: Sarah is on stage
  as herself in this scene.
- **MARIE-LOUISE**: live. A salonnière loyal to King Louis XVIII. Seated, with a fan and
  a letter.
- **GÉRICAULT**: live. The painter of *Le Radeau de la Méduse*. Works at a half-finished
  canvas.
- **SARAH**: live. A witness moving between the worlds.

## Era law

**Nothing in any Scene 9 world is newer than 1819.**

- The wreck: July 1816. The court martial: 1817. Géricault painted the *Raft* in 1818–19,
  and it was shown at the Paris Salon of 1819.
- **France under the Restoration** (Louis XVIII). The Bourbons are back, and royalist
  taste favours pre-Revolution grandeur.
- The script calls the salon "an ornate Louis XIV Salon." Read that as the room's
  **decorative style**: an old château interior in grand Baroque/Rococo taste, still in
  use in 1817. No Empire or Napoleonic motifs. For a royalist household, those would be
  the enemy's.

## History the plates can draw on

These are for authenticity, not literal staging. Verify any of them before putting it in
a prompt as fact.

- In 1818 Géricault rented a large studio in the Faubourg-du-Roule and worked there almost
  as a recluse. He shaved his head to keep himself from going out.
- He brought body parts from the hospital morgue into the studio to study decay. The
  script's rotting head comes from this.
- He had a scale model of the raft built by the ship's carpenter, one of the survivors.
- The finished canvas is about **4.9 × 7.2 m**. It's larger than our centre surface (6 ×
  3.6 m), so the half-finished painting on the studio's back wall can be shown at close to
  true scale, cropped.
- In the real court martial Chaumareys was convicted on some counts and jailed. The play's
  acquittal is dramatic licence, and **the play's version is canon here**.
